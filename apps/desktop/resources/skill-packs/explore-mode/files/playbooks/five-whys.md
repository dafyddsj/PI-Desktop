# Five Whys

Walk back from a problem to its causes by asking "why?" one step at a time. The learner answers, because it is their situation. EXplore asks the next question, checks each link, and keeps the chain honest.

**Why:** Five Whys is simple and useful, and it is easy to misuse. Used carelessly, it produces one tidy causal chain for a problem with several causes, stops at "someone made a mistake", or forces an answer at step five because the method says five. Used carefully, it helps the learner move from symptoms to causes they can act on.

## When to use

- A problem, failure, or recurring pattern in the learner's practice, organisation, or project.
- The `why` request, or `analyse` when the question is causal and local.

It is not suited to large, contested social questions. Those need **systems-thinking** and evidence, not a single chain.

## Method

1. **Define the problem concretely.** Make it observable, specific, and neutral. "Three of five pilot classes stopped using the tool by week four" is a better starting point than "teachers don't like AI".
2. **Ask one why.** "Why did that happen?" The learner answers. Wait for the answer before asking again.
3. **Check the link.** For each answer, ask what shows it, and label the link:
   - **evidenced:** someone observed it or it was measured;
   - **reported:** someone said so;
   - **hypothesised:** it seems likely.

   Ask whether it fully explains the step above, or only partly.
4. **Allow branches.** If there is more than one plausible cause, record each as a branch and let the learner choose which to follow first. Don't force a single chain.
5. **Keep going while it's useful.** Stop when you reach one of these:
   - a cause the learner can act on;
   - a cause outside anyone's control;
   - the point where evidence runs out.

   That may be three whys or seven. Say why you stopped.
6. **Look past the person.** When an answer is "someone didn't…", ask what about the situation made that likely: training, time, incentives, design, information. The aim is to understand causes, not to allocate blame.
7. **Summarise the causal tree.** Show each link with its evidence label. Highlight hypothesised links that deserve checking before anyone acts on them.
8. **Return the conclusions.** The learner decides which causes are the root ones and what, if anything, to do. Record consequential decisions (**show-me-your-work**).

## Output

A causal chain or tree in the learner's words, with an evidence label on each link, the stopping reasons, links to verify, and any learner decisions.

## Watch for

- Answering the whys for the learner.
- A single chain for a multi-cause problem.
- Stopping at blame or at "human error".
- Treating a plausible story as an established cause.
- Continuing past usefulness to reach five.

## Source

Originated in Toyota's production practice and is commonly attributed to Taiichi Ohno. Its known limitations include single-chain oversimplification, dependence on what participants already know, and stopping at symptoms or blame. This playbook is designed to counter those.
