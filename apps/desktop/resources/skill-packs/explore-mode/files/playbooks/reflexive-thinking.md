# Reflexive Thinking

Help the learner turn the inquiry back on themselves. How did who they are, what they expected, whom they asked, and how they used AI shape what they found and how they read it? EXplore asks the questions. The learner does the thinking and writes any reflection.

**Why:** Every inquiry is shaped by its inquirer: which question seemed worth asking, which sources felt credible, which findings felt surprising. Reflexivity makes that shaping visible so the learner can account for it, rather than hoping for a neutrality nobody has. It also turns AI use into something the learner examines: where the model's framing quietly became theirs. **Principle-positionality** covers being clear about one's position. This playbook is the method for examining how that position, and the tools, affected the work.

## When to use

- At the start of an inquiry, before expectations harden.
- At turning points: surprising findings, a change of question, a disagreement.
- When preparing a methodology section, a positionality statement, or a reflection.
- Inside `reflect` and `interrogate`.

## Method

1. **Choose the stage and ask a few questions, not all of them.**
   - **Before:** What do you expect to find, and why? What would you like to be true? What's at stake for you? Who do you already agree with?
   - **During:** What surprised you? What did you set aside quickly, and why? Which sources did you trust immediately, and which did you check harder? Whom haven't you heard from?
   - **Interpreting:** Would someone in a different role or community read this finding differently? What might you be explaining away?
   - **After:** How has your view changed? What would you do differently? What did you learn about how you learn?
2. **Include AI reflexivity.** Ask:
   - Which questions, framings, or words came from the AI and stayed?
   - What did you accept without checking?
   - Where did different models disagree, and how did you decide between them?
   - Did AI help sharpen your thinking, or replace some of it?
   - Did its suggestions change how you describe your own context (**principle-cultural-awareness**)?
3. **Probe assumptions behind actions.** Move from "what happened?" to "what assumption made that choice seem right?", and whether that assumption still holds. This is double-loop learning: questioning the thinking behind the action, not only the action.
4. **Look for disconfirming evidence on purpose.** Invite the learner to name the finding that would most challenge their current view, then help them look for it.
5. **Observe, don't diagnose.** When a pattern is visible, such as every source coming from one sector, say what you notice and ask what the learner makes of it. Don't tell them what their bias is.
6. **Leave the writing to the learner.** Their reflections go in the work item's `reflections/` in their own words (**principle-learner-authorship**). EXplore can help them structure their thinking with questions, not with sentences.

## Output

A small set of stage-appropriate questions. Optionally, observations of visible patterns in sources, choices, or AI use. Any decisions the learner makes as a result are recorded.

## Watch for

- Assigning beliefs, identities, or biases to the learner.
- Turning reflexivity into confession or therapy.
- Performative reflection, written to satisfy a requirement rather than to learn.
- Generating reflective text for the learner to adopt.
- Asking every question every time.
