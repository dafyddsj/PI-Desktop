# Question Framing

Help the learner turn an interest into a question they can actually investigate. Offer framings and their trade-offs. The learner chooses.

**Why:** The question decides what counts as evidence, which sources matter, and what a good answer looks like. A question that is too broad can't be answered in the time available. A question that presupposes its answer ("How will AI revolutionise teaching?") produces advocacy, not inquiry. An agent that quietly swaps in a question it finds easier or more interesting has taken over the inquiry (**principle-human-judgement**).

## When to use

- The start of `research`, `interrogate`, or `compare`, or when the learner says "I want to look into…".
- A question is too broad, vague, loaded, or unanswerable with accessible evidence.
- The inquiry has drifted: new findings have made the original question the wrong one.

Skip it when the learner already has a clear, scoped question. Don't re-open a question the learner has deliberately settled.

## Method

1. **Capture the interest in the learner's words.** Record it verbatim. Every later framing should trace back to it.
2. **Establish the purpose.** What is the inquiry for: an assessment brief, a workplace decision, a practice problem, curiosity? Who is the audience? What time, length, and access constraints apply? Read the brief and rubric if there is one (**principle-follow-applicable-rules**).
3. **Clarify the key terms.** Which words carry the question, and what does the learner mean by them? "AI" could mean generative chatbots, agentic systems, or machine learning in general. Note terms that sources define differently.
4. **Name the claim type the question asks for:**
   - **Descriptive:** what is happening?
   - **Explanatory:** why or how does it happen?
   - **Predictive:** what will happen?
   - **Evaluative or normative:** is it good, or what should be done?
   - **Interpretive:** what does it mean, and for whom?

   Each needs different evidence. A normative question needs values as well as data. A predictive question can't be "proven".
5. **Surface the assumptions.** What does the question take for granted: that something is happening, that it is new, that it is good or bad, that a particular group is the one that matters?
6. **Identify affected parties and standpoint.** Who is affected, whose view does the question start from, and whose is missing (**principle-positionality**, **principle-cultural-awareness**)?
7. **Set the scope.** Population or setting, place, period, and what is deliberately out of scope. Structured frames can help when they fit the field. For example:
   - **PICO** (population, intervention, comparison, outcome) for intervention questions.
   - **PEO** (population, exposure, outcome) for questions about an exposure or experience.
   - **SPIDER** (sample, phenomenon of interest, design, evaluation, research type) for qualitative questions.

   Use a frame only if it helps.
8. **Test answerability.** What evidence could answer this, is it accessible in the time available, and what finding would change the learner's mind? If nothing could, the question needs reworking.
9. **Offer two to four framings.** Present a narrower, a broader, and a differently angled version where useful, each with what it would involve and what it would leave out. Don't rank them into a single "best" question.
10. **Return the choice.** The learner picks, adapts, or rejects. Record the chosen question, and the framings considered with the learner's reasons, in `DECISIONS.md` (**show-me-your-work**).

## Output

A question brief:
- the chosen question and any sub-questions;
- key terms and working definitions;
- the claim type;
- scope in and out;
- assumptions to keep in view;
- affected parties;
- a sketch of the evidence that could answer it;
- known unknowns.

## Watch for

- Substituting the agent's preferred question for the learner's.
- Narrowing until the question is trivial, or keeping it so broad that any answer fits.
- Questions that presuppose their answer, or use leading language.
- A question that has drifted without anyone deciding to change it. Surface the drift, and let the learner decide.
