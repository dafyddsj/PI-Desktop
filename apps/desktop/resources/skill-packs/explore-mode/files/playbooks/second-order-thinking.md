# Second-Order Thinking

Follow consequences past the first one. If this works, then what? If everyone does it, then what? Who adapts, who loses, and what happens later? Keep every step labelled as evidenced, plausible, or speculative.

**Why:** First-order effects are what advocates promise and critics attack. The consequences that matter most often come later and indirectly: people change behaviour in response, incentives shift, and costs land on people who weren't in the original framing. Tracing them helps the learner reason about emerging technology beyond hype and alarm. It doesn't let anyone predict the future. The learner decides which consequences they find convincing (**principle-human-judgement**).

## When to use

- Assessing a proposal, intervention, technology adoption, or policy.
- `challenge` and `analyse`, when the debate is stuck on immediate pros and cons.
- The learner's own plans: "what might this decision set in motion?".

## Method

1. **State the intervention and its intended first-order effect** in one or two sentences. Be specific about who does what.
2. **Ask "if it succeeds, then what?"** Trace the next effect, and the one after that. Stop when the chain runs out of support.
3. **Ask "if it scales, then what?"** What changes when many people, organisations, or systems adopt it? Effects that are harmless for one can be significant for all.
4. **Find actors outside the framing.** Who is affected but wasn't in the original picture: workers, students, communities, competitors, regulators, future users?
5. **Trace incentives and adaptation.** What new incentives does it create? How will people change their behaviour, including in ways that game it, route around it, or weaken the original effect?
6. **Look across time.** What happens in the short, medium, and long term? What is reversible, and what locks in?
7. **Look at distribution.** Who gains and who loses at each step? Do benefits and costs fall on different people?
8. **Label each consequence:**

   | Label | Meaning |
   | --- | --- |
   | Evidenced | Observed in comparable cases, with a source |
   | Plausible | Follows a clear mechanism but hasn't been observed here |
   | Speculative | Possible, with thin support |

   Include positive and negative consequences.
9. **Name signals to watch.** What early evidence would show whether a consequence is starting to happen?
10. **Return the map.** Present the most consequential chains and the key uncertainties. The learner judges which consequences matter and how to weigh them.

## Output

A consequence tree or table. For each consequence: its order, the consequence, the mechanism, who is affected, the time horizon, the evidence label and source, and signals to watch.

## Watch for

- Turning a plausible chain into a forecast.
- Doom or hype spirals: only negative, or only positive, chains.
- Chains where each step is individually plausible but the whole is very unlikely.
- Forgetting the people outside the original framing.
- Endless branching. Prioritise the chains that change the decision.
