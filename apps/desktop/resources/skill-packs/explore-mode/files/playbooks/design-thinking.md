# Design Thinking

Help the learner work on a problem from the perspective of the people who live with it: understand their needs, reframe the problem, explore options widely, and test assumptions with quick prototypes. EXplore can structure the process and challenge assumptions. It cannot supply the human understanding the process depends on.

**Why:** Design thinking is only as good as its contact with real people. A model can produce convincing personas, empathy maps, and "user insights" in seconds, and every one of them is fiction. Treating generated insights as findings is the design equivalent of a fabricated citation (**principle-ai-is-not-evidence**, **principle-do-no-harm**). Used well, AI helps the learner plan research, question assumptions, and widen the option space.

## When to use

- A practice, service, or product problem where the needs of the people involved are central.
- The learner's course or project uses design thinking, or a challenge-based brief invites it.
- Within `research`, `analyse`, or `compare` when options need generating and testing.

If the learner's course defines a particular design thinking model, use that model's stages and language instead of the generic one below.

## Method

Design thinking is commonly described in two ways: as alternating divergent and convergent phases (the Design Council's Double Diamond: discover, define, develop, deliver), or as modes (the Stanford d.school: empathise, define, ideate, prototype, test). Both are iterative. Move back whenever something is learned.

1. **Discover: understand people and context.** Help the learner plan how to learn from the people involved: whom to talk to, what to observe, and what existing evidence to read. Engaging people may need permission or ethics approval (**principle-follow-applicable-rules**, **principle-protect-data**). The learner gathers the insight. EXplore can help organise and question what they bring back.
2. **Define: frame the problem.** Synthesise what was learned into a problem statement grounded in real needs, often phrased as "How might we…?". Separate observed needs from assumed ones. Check whose needs are centred and whose are missing (**principle-cultural-awareness**).
3. **Develop: generate options.** Widen before narrowing. EXplore can suggest options, analogies from other fields, and provocations. Label them as generated ideas, not recommendations. The learner selects.
4. **Prototype: make assumptions testable.** Turn an option into the cheapest thing that can test its riskiest assumption: a sketch, storyboard, role-play, mock-up, or script.
5. **Test: learn from people.** Plan how to test with real people, and what result would change the design. Record what was learned, including what failed.
6. **Iterate and record.** Keep an assumption log (assumption, how tested, result). Record the learner's design decisions (**show-me-your-work**).

## Output

Depending on the stage:
- a research plan;
- a problem statement with evidence for each need;
- an option set labelled as generated or learner-sourced;
- prototype and test plans;
- an assumption log;
- decisions.

## Watch for

- Personas, empathy maps, or quotes generated by AI and presented as research.
- Jumping to solutions before understanding the problem.
- Treating one round as finished.
- Designing for an imagined "average user" who stands in for everyone.
- Engaging people without the permissions their involvement requires.
