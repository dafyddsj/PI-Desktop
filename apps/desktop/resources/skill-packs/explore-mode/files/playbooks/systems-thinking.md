# Systems Thinking

Look at the structure that produces the behaviour, not just the event. Map who and what is involved, how they affect each other, where effects loop back, where they are delayed, and where a small change might matter. Say which links are evidenced and which are guesses.

**Why:** Emerging technologies rarely have single, direct effects. They land in organisations, markets, and communities with their own incentives and feedback, and the obvious fix often produces the opposite of what was intended. A systems map helps the learner see those dynamics. A confident-looking diagram of guessed relationships is also a very persuasive way to present speculation as fact, so evidence labels are not optional.

## When to use

- Explaining why a pattern persists or why interventions keep failing.
- Anticipating how a technology, policy, or practice will play out beyond its first effect.
- Comparing interventions by where they act on a system.
- As a method within `analyse`, `challenge`, `compare`, or `research`.

Not every question needs a system map. A narrow factual question doesn't.

## Method

1. **Set the purpose and boundary.** What behaviour or outcome are we trying to understand? What is inside the system and what is outside? Boundaries are choices. Name who drew them, and who or what they leave out (**principle-positionality**).
2. **List the elements:** actors (people, groups, organisations), resources, rules and norms, technologies, and information flows.
3. **Map the relationships.** For each link, record its direction ("more A leads to more B", or "…to less B") and its evidence status:
   - **evidenced**, with a source ID;
   - **reported**, meaning it has a source but the source is unverified;
   - **hypothesised**.
4. **Find the feedback loops.**
   - **Reinforcing loops** amplify change: growth, lock-in, vicious cycles.
   - **Balancing loops** resist change: correction, saturation, pushback.

   Name each loop and what drives it.
5. **Note delays and stocks.** Where do effects take time to appear? What accumulates or drains, such as skills, trust, data, or debt? Delays explain many overshoots and failed interventions.
6. **Surface incentives and power.** Who benefits from the current behaviour? Who can change the rules? Whose interests shape the goals of the system?
7. **Consider leverage points.** Where might an intervention have outsized effect? Changes to parameters (numbers, targets) are usually weaker than changes to feedback, information flows, rules, goals, or the mindset the system serves. Treat any leverage claim as a hypothesis to test.
8. **Stress-test the map.** Ask "what happens if…?" for a few changes. Check that the map explains the behaviour you started with. Add the learner's knowledge and remove links nobody can support.
9. **Return the map with its uncertainty.** List the key loops, candidate leverage points, and the links most in need of evidence. The learner decides what the map means for their inquiry.

## Output

A system map as a structured list, or a diagram (for example, Mermaid) with labelled links. Every link shows its polarity and evidence status. Include named loops, delays, a note on incentives and power, candidate leverage points, and the highest-priority uncertainties.

## Watch for

- Mapping everything. A map with sixty links explains nothing.
- Unfalsifiable diagrams: loops that could explain any outcome.
- Presenting hypothesised links as findings.
- Single-cause stories dressed up as systems.
- Ignoring who set the boundary and who sits outside it.

## Source
